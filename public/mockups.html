Design systems don't scale — teams do.

After seven years building component libraries at two companies, the bottleneck was never the tokens. It was the conversation about when to extend them.

BH
Brian Hall
Senior Software Engineer · M.S. HCI

When I joined NFI in 2017 we had no design system. By 2021 we had a component library in Phoenix LiveView and Tailwind with 80+ components, documentation, and a Figma kit. That's the story you'd tell at a conference. Here's the other story.

The tokens were never the problem. The spacing scale was fine. The color ramp was fine. What we kept running into was a question that no design system documentation ever answers: is this a new pattern, or is this an existing pattern pushed into an edge case?

"The question isn't whether your tokens are right. It's whether your team can agree on what 'right' means fast enough to ship."

The engineers I worked with were excellent. They cared about consistency. But when a new screen landed in the backlog, the conversation that needed to happen — does this new table variant belong in the system or is it a one-off? — was taking 30–40 minutes per ticket. Multiply that across a two-week sprint and you've lost a day of velocity to design governance.

The pattern that actually fixed it

We introduced what I called a "30-second rule." Before any new component got added to the library, the proposing engineer had to answer three questions out loud in standup:

  1. Where does this component appear in the product today, and where will it appear in the next quarter?
  2. Which existing component is closest, and what specifically makes it wrong for this use?
  3. If we add this, what do we deprecate or what constraint do we add to prevent misuse?

It sounds obvious. It wasn't. Most teams I'd seen treat the design system like a Lego bin — add pieces freely, never remove them. The discipline of answering those three questions shifted the conversation from "can we add this" to "should we add this." That's the entire game.

Note

This only works if the system has a steward — someone who owns the "no." Without that, the three questions become a formality and the library bloats anyway. At NFI that was me for two years, then a rotating responsibility across senior engineers.

What I'd do differently

I'd document the decisions, not just the components. Every component in our library had props documentation. Almost none had a record of what we decided not to build and why. When engineers left and new ones joined, that institutional memory walked out the door with them.

A design system is a set of bets. Document the bets. Future-you will want to know if the bet paid off.

01 Exception filter placement

Sidebar panel
Filter lived in a right-hand drawer. Required an extra click to open. 11 of 12 participants in usability testing didn't find it on first try.
✓ Chosen
Top filter bar
Persistent, always visible. Matches the left-to-right scan pattern observed in contextual inquiry. Cut ~3 clicks per exception workflow.
Why

Contextual inquiry revealed coordinators spend 40% of exception time just locating the right filter. Proximity to the list is what mattered.

02 Priority exception surfacing
+
✕ Ruled out
Badge count in nav
Shows a number but requires navigation. Users still had to scan the full list to find the right item.
✓ Chosen
Pinned row + brass border
Highest priority exception always at position 1, visually distinct. No scanning required — the right item is always first.
Why

Cognitive task analysis showed triage was the most mentally demanding step. Eliminating the scan step was the highest-leverage change available.
